Phase is where most intuition fails. On the pulse train, watch the phase flip by 180° every time the magnitude envelope crosses zero: that sign change is the sinc going negative, and discarding it would misplace every pulse edge.

Unscaled, every line shrinks like 1/T0 and the spectrum flattens toward nothing — which looks like information being destroyed. It isn't; the power is spreading across more lines. Multiplying by T0 converts amplitude-per-line into amplitude per unit frequency. That is why X(ω) is a spectral density with units of amplitude·time, not an amplitude.

Change τ and the envelope moves. Change T0 and it doesn't. The envelope belongs to the pulse, not to the repetition.

Discreteness in one domain forces periodicity in the other. Read any cell backwards and it names its own spectrum.

This is not a mnemonic, it is panel 02 run in reverse. There, sampling the time axis at spacing T0 made the spectrum a set of lines. The theorem is symmetric: sampling the frequency axis at spacing f0 periodizes the time signal with period 1/f0.

Which is exactly what the DFT does to your data without asking. It sees your record as one period of something infinitely repeating. If the record doesn't hold a whole number of cycles, the repetition has a discontinuity at the seam — and the energy that jump injects is what panel 05 calls leakage.

Push fsig past the shaded Nyquist band. Nothing dramatic happens in the time plot — the samples are still perfectly good numbers. What changes is that a lower sinusoid now passes through every one of them, and no amount of processing can tell you which one was there. The replica from the neighboring copy has landed inside your band and become indistinguishable from a real signal.

The apparent frequency is |f_sig − f_s·round(f_sig/f_s)|. Watch it fold back and forth as you sweep — that reflection off the Nyquist edge is why the effect is drawn as a folding diagram in older texts.

Set k0 to exactly 10 with no window: a single bin, everything else at the numerical floor. Nudge to 10.5 and the floor rises by fifty-odd decibels. Nothing changed about the signal — only about whether the periodic extension is continuous at the seam.

Now switch windows at k0 = 10.5. Sidelobes drop hard, and the mainlobe widens in exchange. That trade is the whole design space: rectangular resolves closely spaced equal-amplitude tones best, Blackman–Harris is what you want when a small tone sits near a large one. The 1/n coefficient decay you watched on the square wave in panel 01 is this same discontinuity penalty, seen from the other side.
